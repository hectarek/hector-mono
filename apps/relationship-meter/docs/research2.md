# Understanding the Science of Relationships

## Key Factors in Relationships

Scientific research shows that several key variables define and influence interpersonal relationships. These include both **behavioral factors** (how people interact) and **emotional factors** (how people feel toward each other):

* **Frequency of Interaction:** Close relationships typically involve frequent contact or time spent together. In fact, a classic measure of closeness, the Relationship Closeness Inventory (RCI), assesses how often partners see each other[\[1\]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4466912/#:~:text=on%20each%20other%3B%20the%20degree,subscales%20in%20the%20methods%20section). Strong ties usually involve more regular communication than weak ties.

* **Diversity of Shared Activities:** The breadth of experiences shared is another factor. The RCI includes how many different activities or contexts two people engage in together[\[1\]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4466912/#:~:text=on%20each%20other%3B%20the%20degree,subscales%20in%20the%20methods%20section). For example, two friends who not only chat but also work out, travel, and collaborate on projects together have a more multifaceted (and often stronger) relationship.

* **Emotional Intensity and Intimacy:** Strong relationships are marked by emotional closeness – trust, mutual care, and the sharing of personal thoughts. Granovetter’s influential theory of *tie strength* in social networks defines a tie’s strength as partly a function of emotional intensity and intimacy (mutual confiding) between people[\[2\]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11850326/#:~:text=The%20strength%20of%20a%20tie,media%20usage%20intensity%20index%20was). In close friendships or romantic bonds, individuals feel deeply connected and comfortable sharing vulnerabilities.

* **Reciprocal Support:** Healthy relationships involve mutual support and reciprocity. Granovetter’s definition also highlights **reciprocal services**, meaning the give-and-take of help or favors, as a core component of relationship strength[\[2\]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11850326/#:~:text=The%20strength%20of%20a%20tie,media%20usage%20intensity%20index%20was). Strong relationships tend to have balanced exchange – both people contribute to and benefit from the bond.

* **Influence and Interdependence:** In close relationships, people affect each other’s decisions and well-being. Psychologists describe **interdependence** as a hallmark of closeness – partners’ lives become interconnected. The RCI’s “strength” subscale measures how much influence each person has on the other’s daily activities and future plans[\[1\]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4466912/#:~:text=on%20each%20other%3B%20the%20degree,subscales%20in%20the%20methods%20section). High interdependence (e.g. consulting each other on important decisions) is a sign of a strong, involved relationship.

Other qualities often studied include **trust** and **commitment**, especially in romantic or long-term relationships. High trust and commitment correlate with relationship stability and satisfaction in couples, and similar elements of reliability and loyalty play a role in friendships as well. In short, factors such as how **often** you interact, in **how many ways** you connect, and with what level of **emotional intimacy, support, and mutual influence** all contribute to the scientific understanding of relationship strength[\[1\]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4466912/#:~:text=on%20each%20other%3B%20the%20degree,subscales%20in%20the%20methods%20section)[\[2\]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11850326/#:~:text=The%20strength%20of%20a%20tie,media%20usage%20intensity%20index%20was).

## Measuring Relationship Strength

Determining the strength or closeness of a relationship can be done in both subjective and objective ways. Researchers have developed various **scales and metrics** to quantify how strong a social tie is:

![][image1]  
*Illustration of the Inclusion-of-Other-in-Self (IOS) scale, a simple pictorial measure of perceived relationship closeness. Higher overlap between “You” and “Other” circles indicates greater closeness[\[3\]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4466912/#:~:text=refers%20to%20the%20respondent%20and,completely%20overlapping%20set%20of%20circles).*

* **Self-Report Closeness Scales:** One widely used tool is the *Inclusion of the Other in the Self (IOS)* scale, which asks a person to choose between diagrams of two circles (self and other) overlapping to varying degrees[\[3\]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4466912/#:~:text=refers%20to%20the%20respondent%20and,completely%20overlapping%20set%20of%20circles). The more overlap chosen, the closer the person feels the relationship to be. Despite its simplicity, the IOS correlates strongly with more elaborate closeness questionnaires[\[4\]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4466912/#:~:text=One%20central%20observation%20of%20Study,coupled%20with%20the%20simplicity%20and), meaning it effectively captures how bonded someone feels with another. Similarly, the RCI mentioned earlier provides a composite closeness score by combining frequency of contact, diversity of activities, and strength of influence[\[1\]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4466912/#:~:text=on%20each%20other%3B%20the%20degree,subscales%20in%20the%20methods%20section). These self-report measures gauge the *perceived* closeness and importance of the relationship to the individual.

* **Behavioral Indicators:** Apart from questionnaires, researchers also look at observable indicators of tie strength. **Contact frequency** (how often people communicate or meet) and **recentness of interaction** are straightforward measures – a relationship where you talk every day is generally stronger than one with annual contact. **Duration of the relationship** (how long you’ve known each other) can matter too, as long-standing relationships often have deeper history to draw on. Additionally, the **emotional tone** of interactions (positive vs. negative) is relevant; frequent conflict or negativity can weaken relational strength despite high contact.

* **Network Theory Metrics:** In social network analysis, *relationship strength* is often judged by multiple criteria. As noted, Granovetter’s classic definition includes the **time spent together, emotional intensity, intimacy, and reciprocity** of a tie[\[2\]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11850326/#:~:text=The%20strength%20of%20a%20tie,media%20usage%20intensity%20index%20was). A “strong tie” (e.g. a best friend or close family member) scores high on all these dimensions, whereas a “weak tie” (an acquaintance) might involve infrequent, superficial contact. Network researchers sometimes measure the **communication frequency** (e.g. call or message counts) and the **content** (personal vs. impersonal) to classify ties. For instance, one study of phone records found that close friendships could be identified by very frequent calls or messages, whereas distant connections had sparse communication.

In sum, the strength of a relationship can be determined by asking people how close they feel and by looking at how they behave: how often they interact, how deeply they share, and how much they support and influence each other. Strong relationships generally show **high levels of ongoing interaction, emotional closeness, and mutual support**, whereas weaker ones show fewer interactions or more limited emotional exchange[\[2\]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11850326/#:~:text=The%20strength%20of%20a%20tie,media%20usage%20intensity%20index%20was). By combining subjective ratings with objective interaction data, scientists can get a comprehensive picture of relationship strength.

## Challenges in Maintaining Relationships

It’s important to recognize that not everyone finds it easy to maintain relationships. There are certain conditions or personal traits that can make keeping up with social ties difficult (even if the person isn’t “antisocial” or intentionally avoiding others):

* **Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD):** Adults with ADHD often struggle with inattention and forgetfulness, which can impact their social life. Research shows that greater ADHD symptom severity is linked to *impairments in interpersonal relationships*[\[5\]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10399076/#:~:text=Association%2C%202013%20%29,33%20Schrevel%20et%20al). Many adults with ADHD report difficulty **maintaining long-term relationships** – not due to lack of caring, but because they may be disorganized, easily distracted, or lose track of time. They might forget to return calls or fail to schedule meet-ups, causing friendships to dwindle unintentionally. Qualitative studies have noted that adults with ADHD truly desire friendships but often feel they aren’t “keeping up” well, leading to guilt or anxiety[\[6\]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10399076/#:~:text=2015%20%3B%20Cho%20%26%20Park%2C,that%20one%20possible%20cause%20of).

* **Autism Spectrum Conditions:** Individuals on the autism spectrum (including those with Asperger’s, for example) can also have a hard time with relationship maintenance. Autism is characterized by difficulties with social interaction and communication that *persist into adulthood*, often resulting in social isolation or limited close friendships[\[7\]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8788910/#:~:text=Difficulties%20with%20social%20interactions%20and,participation%20activities%20and%20community%20contacts). Crucially, this isn’t because autistic people don’t care about others – many *do* want friends – but they may struggle with the unwritten rules of socializing, such as knowing when to reach out, how often to check in, or how to read subtle social cues. Consequently, they might not initiate contact as frequently as others, which can make relationships fade if the other party doesn’t take the lead.

* **Social Anxiety or Shyness:** While not a formal “condition” in the same way, high social anxiety can cause people to withdraw or avoid initiating contact out of fear of bothering others or being rejected. These individuals might deeply value their friends but find themselves *overthinking* the act of reaching out, sometimes leading them to unintentionally neglect the relationship. (For example, an anxious person might wait for the other to call first, and if both parties do this, they end up not talking for long periods.)

* **Busy Lifestyles and Cognitive Load:** Even without a specific disorder, some people simply struggle to *think about* keeping up with friends amid a hectic life. Demanding work schedules, family responsibilities, or mental fatigue can crowd out social intentions. Psychologists note that maintaining friendships requires active effort – planning get-togethers, remembering to check in – which can feel burdensome when one is stressed or busy[\[8\]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7483134/#:~:text=Social%20engagement%20may%20be%20an,friends%20versus%20family%20and%20cognition). This is one reason adults often lose touch with friends: not due to ill will, but because life distractions reduce the mental energy available for social maintenance.

It’s worth emphasizing that these challenges are not about being *truly antisocial* (in the sense of disliking others or lacking empathy). Rather, people may genuinely value their relationships yet struggle with the **executive functioning and emotional bandwidth** needed to consistently nurture those ties. Recognizing these hurdles is important when designing a “relationship meter” app – such a tool could serve as a helpful reminder or motivator for people who want to stay connected but find it hard to do so spontaneously.

## Qualities and Properties to Consider in a Relationship-Tracking System

When building a system like a “relationship meter,” we should incorporate the scientific understanding of relationship properties to make it effective and realistic. Key considerations include:

* **Different Types of Relationships:** Not all relationships behave the same way. Friendships, family relationships, and romantic partnerships have different typical dynamics. For example, *friendships* are voluntary and often require active maintenance through shared activities and communication[\[8\]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7483134/#:~:text=Social%20engagement%20may%20be%20an,friends%20versus%20family%20and%20cognition). In contrast, *family ties* (like with parents or siblings) often have a baseline of commitment or obligation that can keep them intact even with lower contact frequency. *Romantic relationships* usually involve additional dimensions like physical intimacy and explicit commitment. Your system might allow users to categorize relationships (friend, family, partner, colleague, etc.) because the “meter” might decay at different rates depending on the type. (A best friend or significant other likely merits more frequent interaction than a casual coworker acquaintance.)

* **Baseline Closeness and Intimacy:** Each relationship starts with a certain strength or closeness level, and this affects how quickly it might “fade” without contact. A new acquaintance could drop off your radar in weeks if you don’t interact, whereas a childhood best friend might remain emotionally close for months or even years with little contact, due to the strong foundation. In other words, relationships have *inertia*: very close bonds are more resilient to time apart. The system could account for this by weighting the meter differently – a higher initial closeness means a slower decay, perhaps – reflecting that a strong bond endures.

* **Frequency and Recency of Interaction:** Empirical evidence suggests that **regular contact is crucial to maintaining friendship strength**[\[8\]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7483134/#:~:text=Social%20engagement%20may%20be%20an,friends%20versus%20family%20and%20cognition). The app’s meter should probably tick downward as time since last interaction increases. You might consider a model where each interaction “boosts” the meter (depending on the depth of interaction), and conversely, long periods of no contact cause a gradual decline. What counts as an “interaction” could be customizable (a meaningful phone call or in-person meet-up might raise the meter more than a mere social media like). This aligns with research showing that spending time together and engaging in shared activities strengthens bonds.

* **Layers of Closeness (Strong vs. Weak Ties):** Humans naturally organize their social networks into layers of closeness. On average, people have a very small inner circle of about 5 intimate relationships, around 15 “good” friends, 50 broader friends, and up to about 150 total meaningful contacts (often called *Dunbar’s number*)[\[9\]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-021-90984-z?error=cookies_not_supported&code=838866db-843f-4f3a-89e3-8bc955fc13f9#:~:text=with%20typical%20sizes%20varying%20in,circle%20is%20in). Each layer represents a drop in emotional closeness and typically in contact frequency. This has implications for your system: it might be overwhelming to treat all contacts the same way. Instead, the app might focus on the closer layers (e.g. close friends/family) with higher maintenance needs, or allow users to prioritize certain relationships. It could also remind users that it’s normal to have different interaction frequency expectations for different circles – e.g. you might aim to see your 5 closest friends every week, your next 10-15 friends roughly monthly, and so on[\[10\]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-021-90984-z?error=cookies_not_supported&code=838866db-843f-4f3a-89e3-8bc955fc13f9#:~:text=quality13%20%2C%20%2042%2C%2018,whose%20death%20would%20cause%20great)[\[11\]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-021-90984-z?error=cookies_not_supported&code=838866db-843f-4f3a-89e3-8bc955fc13f9#:~:text=exhibit%20distinct%20sizes%20%28around%201,45%20that%20the%20existence%20of). Integrating this science prevents unrealistic expectations (nobody can constantly engage all 150 of their contacts).

* **Mutuality and Feedback:** A relationship isn’t one-sided; the other person’s engagement matters too. While your app can only track the user’s actions, it might consider prompting *reciprocity*. For instance, if the meter is low despite the user reaching out a lot (perhaps the friend isn’t responding), that indicates a different problem (one-sided effort) that a simple meter might not fix. In research terms, high-quality relationships have reciprocal influence and support[\[2\]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11850326/#:~:text=The%20strength%20of%20a%20tie,media%20usage%20intensity%20index%20was). Perhaps the system could encourage the user to reflect on whether both sides are investing in the friendship, or even suggest reaching out and explicitly checking in with the other person about the relationship.

* **Qualitative Aspects (Trust, Support, Emotions):** Beyond frequency of contact, remember that *how* you interact matters. Two people could see each other daily but have shallow or hostile interactions – their relationship meter shouldn’t be maxed out just by sheer contact. Qualitative properties like **trust, emotional support, positive sentiment, and low conflict** are crucial for a strong relationship. While these are harder to quantify, a sophisticated system might incorporate proxies (for example, a journal feature for the user to rate how they felt about recent interactions). Academic studies on relationship quality often emphasize **satisfaction, trust, and commitment** as core components of strong relationships. Even if your app doesn’t directly measure those, its advice could be informed by them (e.g., reminding users that positive interactions – being supportive, showing appreciation – will strengthen the bond, not just frequent check-ins).

In designing the “relationship meter,” grounding it in empirical research ensures it reflects real social dynamics. People maintain relationships by **investing time and effort**, sharing experiences, and providing mutual care. At the same time, cognitive limits mean we can only maintain so many close ties, and different relationships need different levels of attention[\[9\]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-021-90984-z?error=cookies_not_supported&code=838866db-843f-4f3a-89e3-8bc955fc13f9#:~:text=with%20typical%20sizes%20varying%20in,circle%20is%20in)[\[8\]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7483134/#:~:text=Social%20engagement%20may%20be%20an,friends%20versus%20family%20and%20cognition). By accounting for these factors – type of relationship, current strength, decay over time, and the quality of interactions – your app can help users not just track their relationships, but understand and improve them in a meaningful, science-backed way.
