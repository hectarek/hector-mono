# Scientific Insights on Relationship Dynamics

To inform the design of a **“relationship meter”** app, we can draw on research in psychology and social science about how relationships function. Below, we address key questions about relationships, including the variables that define them, how to gauge relationship strength, why some people struggle with maintaining ties, and what qualities should be considered when building a system to track relationship health.

## Key Variables in Relationships

Research shows that **relationships have multiple dimensions** or variables that contribute to their quality and strength. Some of the most important factors identified in scientific studies include:

* **Emotional Closeness (Intimacy):** The subjective feeling of emotional bonding, mutual confiding, and shared understanding between people. High perceived closeness is a core indicator of a strong tie[\[1\]](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/158612/1/paper_SN_13.pdf#:~:text=%28Granovet%02ter%2C%201977%29,Gilbert) – close friends or partners feel very connected and “in sync” with each other. This intimacy often develops through meaningful conversations and vulnerability over time.

* **Time Spent & Interaction Frequency:** The **amount of time spent together** and **how often people communicate or meet** are powerful predictors of relationship strength. Close relationships typically involve **frequent contact**, and this frequency correlates strongly with emotional closeness[\[2\]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-022-06066-1?error=cookies_not_supported&code=c48bb189-b1f1-43c4-8eab-bef7cb92c190#:~:text=There%20are%2C%20however%2C%20alternative%20ways,the%20role%20of%20first%20and)[\[3\]](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/158612/1/paper_SN_13.pdf#:~:text=spent%20and%20intensity,Gilbert). In fact, studies find that if contact becomes infrequent, the relationship tends to weaken: for example, the **emotional intensity of a friendship drops when contact frequency decreases**[\[4\]](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1090513810000966#:~:text=maintenance%20and%20decay,between%20kin%20relations%20and%20friendships). Regular interactions (e.g. weekly catch-ups or daily messages) help maintain the bond, whereas long periods of no contact can lead to a feeling of distance.

* **Emotional Intensity & Support:** Strong relationships are marked by a high degree of **emotional investment**, such as affection, care, and support during both good and bad times. Emotional intensity refers to how strongly one cares about the other and the extent of shared positive feelings[\[1\]](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/158612/1/paper_SN_13.pdf#:~:text=%28Granovet%02ter%2C%201977%29,Gilbert). Closely related is **social support** – providing help, comfort, and listening when needed. Research shows that positive aspects like intimacy and **supportive behavior** contribute to better relationship outcomes (and even benefit health)[\[5\]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7485933/#:~:text=The%20quality%20of%20close%20relationships,and%20improved)[\[6\]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7485933/#:~:text=%28Mustafa%2C%20Carson,Rovillard%20%26%20Kuiper%2C%202011). In essence, relationships thrive when people genuinely support each other and share significant experiences.

* **Reciprocity (Mutual Exchange):** Healthy relationships generally have a **balanced two-way exchange** – both individuals contribute effort, communicate, and respond to each other’s needs. This **symmetrical reciprocity** (mutual give-and-take) is considered a standard for strong friendship by social psychologists[\[7\]](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1523506/full#:~:text=and%20settings%20%282%29,being%2C%20social%20interactions%2C%20and%20cognitive). In academic terms, **communication reciprocity** (both parties initiating and responding in conversation) is used as an indicator of tie strength[\[8\]](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/158612/1/paper_SN_13.pdf#:~:text=of%20indicators%20to%20measure%20this,Gilbert). When only one person is always reaching out or giving and the other is passive, the relationship is less stable. Thus, maintaining strong ties involves a **mutual commitment** of time and attention from both sides.

* **Trust and Reliability:** **Trust** – the confidence that the other person is reliable, honest, and has your best interests at heart – is widely regarded as a **fundamental component** of close relationships[\[9\]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12316384/#:~:text=Trust%20is%20widely%20regarded%20as,we%20then%20confirmed). High trust allows people to be vulnerable and strengthens their bond. Along with trust, a sense of **commitment** (intentions to continue the relationship into the future) often characterizes strong long-term relationships. In romantic partnerships, for example, trust and commitment go hand-in-hand and predict relationship stability[\[9\]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12316384/#:~:text=Trust%20is%20widely%20regarded%20as,we%20then%20confirmed). In friendships, trusting someone (knowing you can count on them) is key to feeling securely connected.

*Different types of relationships may emphasize these variables differently.* For instance, romantic relationships often involve high intimacy, commitment, and trust, whereas a casual friendship might rely more on enjoyment and moderate contact. However, **all close relationships** generally benefit from **frequent positive interaction, mutual support, and trust** as core ingredients.

## Measuring the Strength of Relationships

Determining or quantifying the **strength of a relationship** is a complex task, because strength is a *latent construct* that isn’t directly observed but inferred from various indicators[\[10\]](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/158612/1/paper_SN_13.pdf#:~:text=ego,Granovetter%2C%202018). Researchers therefore assess relationship strength by evaluating the key factors above through both subjective and objective measures:

* **Self-reported Closeness:** One common approach is simply to ask people how close they feel to someone or to rank the people in their life by closeness. These subjective ratings tend to be very informative – for example, if you rate a friend as “very close,” it typically correlates with objective behaviors like contacting that friend frequently[\[11\]](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/158612/1/paper_SN_13.pdf#:~:text=Data%20distinguishing%20close%20and%20weak,characteristics%20were%20replicated%20by%20Calastri). Psychologists use instruments like the Inclusion of Other in Self (IOS) scale or other closeness scales to quantify this feeling of connectedness.

* **Interaction Frequency and Duration:** Objective measures such as **frequency of contact** (e.g. number of calls or messages, how often you meet) and **time spent together** are used as proxies for tie strength[\[2\]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-022-06066-1?error=cookies_not_supported&code=c48bb189-b1f1-43c4-8eab-bef7cb92c190#:~:text=There%20are%2C%20however%2C%20alternative%20ways,the%20role%20of%20first%20and). A stronger relationship is usually one with **regular, ongoing interaction**. Some studies have leveraged communication logs or social media data – for instance, counting messages or call frequency – to infer which relationships are “strong ties” vs. “weak ties”[\[2\]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-022-06066-1?error=cookies_not_supported&code=c48bb189-b1f1-43c4-8eab-bef7cb92c190#:~:text=There%20are%2C%20however%2C%20alternative%20ways,the%20role%20of%20first%20and). These behavioral metrics provide continuous data on interaction intensity.

* **Composite Indicators:** Because no single factor perfectly captures strength, researchers often combine multiple indicators. As noted, **relationship strength is multifaceted**, so closeness, interaction frequency, reciprocity, and support all serve as **signals of the underlying strength**[\[10\]](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/158612/1/paper_SN_13.pdf#:~:text=ego,Granovetter%2C%202018). For example, one might create a composite score that weighs how often you talk, how emotionally close you feel, and how much support you exchange. Mark Granovetter’s classic theory of tie strength suggested that a combination of **time spent together, emotional intensity, intimacy, and reciprocal services** determines tie strength[\[1\]](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/158612/1/paper_SN_13.pdf#:~:text=%28Granovet%02ter%2C%201977%29,Gilbert). Modern studies build on this by using statistical models to find an underlying “strength” factor that explains people’s patterns of interactions and emotional closeness with each other[\[10\]](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/158612/1/paper_SN_13.pdf#:~:text=ego,Granovetter%2C%202018).

In practical terms, a **strong relationship** tends to show **high values on multiple dimensions** – frequent communication, high mutual trust and support, deep affection, and long-term commitment. A **weaker relationship** might be characterized by infrequent contact, low emotional intimacy, and minimal interdependence. Thus, to **measure the strength** of a given relationship, one would look at **how consistently and intensely the pair interact**, and **how each person feels about the importance of the relationship**. Empirical research confirms that someone you label a “close friend” is likely someone you speak with very often and rely on for support[\[11\]](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/158612/1/paper_SN_13.pdf#:~:text=Data%20distinguishing%20close%20and%20weak,characteristics%20were%20replicated%20by%20Calastri)[\[3\]](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/158612/1/paper_SN_13.pdf#:~:text=spent%20and%20intensity,Gilbert), indicating that subjective closeness aligns with objective interaction strength.

## Challenges in Maintaining Relationships (When It Doesn’t Come Naturally)

Not everyone finds it easy to keep up with relationships. There are indeed **conditions and personality differences** where people struggle with relationship maintenance even if they aren’t deliberately antisocial. These individuals **do want friends or connections**, but may have difficulties in the *practical or cognitive aspects* of maintaining ties. For example:

* **Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD):** Research has shown that people (especially children and adolescents) with ADHD often have trouble sustaining friendships. They tend to have **fewer friends and lower-quality friendships on average**, as well as more uneven social interactions[\[12\]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10406331/#:~:text=quantity%20was%20the%20most%20investigated,two%20had%20strong%20methodological%20quality). This isn’t due to lack of desire for friends, but rather symptoms like inattention, distractibility, and disorganization which can lead them to forget to check in, miss social cues, or behave impulsively. As a result, someone with ADHD might unintentionally neglect to **“water” the friendship** regularly, causing the “friendship meter” to drop despite caring about their friends.

* **Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD):** Autistic individuals can also **struggle with forming and maintaining relationships** because of differences in social communication and understanding. Studies consistently find that the core characteristics of autism affect the **formation and maintenance of friendships across the lifespan**[\[13\]](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1523506/full#:~:text=activities,the%20majority%20of%20their%20years). Autistic adults often deeply desire friendship, but may find it challenging to initiate contact, interpret social signals, or adapt to the unwritten rules of social reciprocity. They may **appear asocial** not because they want to be alone, but because navigating the give-and-take of friendship is anxiety-provoking or cognitively taxing. It has been noted that social difficulties can become even more prominent in adulthood for autistic individuals, once structured school environments (where making friends is easier) are gone[\[13\]](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1523506/full#:~:text=activities,the%20majority%20of%20their%20years). Thus, without conscious effort or support, they might unintentionally let relationships lapse.

* **Other Factors:** Even outside of clinical conditions, some people are simply less adept at relationship maintenance. Personality traits like **introversion** or **high self-focus** might make an individual less likely to reach out proactively. Likewise, someone who is very busy or prone to forgetfulness might unintentionally disconnect from friends. **Social anxiety** can also play a role – a person might fear that reaching out will bother others, leading them to withdraw and lose contact over time. While academic research on these everyday struggles is still emerging, it’s clear that **failing to maintain relationships is often not a matter of willful antisocial behavior, but of psychological barriers or skill deficits**. This is precisely where a relationship-tracking app could help: by reminding users to check in and providing a structure for interactions, it could support those who struggle to keep relationships on their radar.

## Qualities of Relationships to Consider in a Relationship Meter

When designing a system like a **“relationship meter”** that keeps track of social ties, it’s important to account for the **properties of relationships that science has identified**. Relationships are not one-size-fits-all, and a useful system should be sensitive to differences in closeness, type, and needed maintenance. Key considerations include:

* **Relationship Type and Context:** Different kinds of relationships function differently. For instance, **family bonds versus friendships** have distinct dynamics. Research shows that kin (family relationships) are generally **more resilient to lapses in contact** – they don’t weaken as quickly if you don’t talk for a while – whereas **friendships require more maintenance** to remain close[\[4\]](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1090513810000966#:~:text=maintenance%20and%20decay,between%20kin%20relations%20and%20friendships). In a longitudinal study, the emotional intensity of friendships dropped off faster with reduced contact compared to that of kin relationships[\[4\]](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1090513810000966#:~:text=maintenance%20and%20decay,between%20kin%20relations%20and%20friendships). This implies your app might treat certain categories with different decay rates on the “meter.” **Romantic relationships** also have unique patterns (usually high frequency of contact and higher expectations for responsiveness). **Consider categorizing relationships** (family, close friend, acquaintance, etc.) because the **expected interaction frequency and tolerance for gaps** will differ. What counts as neglect for a best friend (not talking for a month) might be normal for a distant cousin.

* **Closeness Tiers (Inner vs. Outer Circle):** People naturally organize their social network into **layers of closeness**, often described by **Dunbar’s circles** (approximately 5 intimate relationships, 15 very close friends, 50 good friends, and so on)[\[14\]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-022-06066-1?error=cookies_not_supported&code=c48bb189-b1f1-43c4-8eab-bef7cb92c190#:~:text=,it%20is%20also%20costly%20in). These circles reflect varying emotional closeness and come with different **time investment** levels – for example, one analysis found we devote about **40% of our social time to our inner \~5 people** and progressively less to larger circles[\[14\]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-022-06066-1?error=cookies_not_supported&code=c48bb189-b1f1-43c4-8eab-bef7cb92c190#:~:text=,it%20is%20also%20costly%20in). In designing the meter, it’s useful to acknowledge that **not all relationships are equal**. A “one-size” meter decay might not fit: your **inner circle relationships** might merit more frequent pings to stay topped up, whereas more casual friendships could have a slower decay. Perhaps allow the user to mark some contacts as higher priority (closer), which the system then requires more frequent interaction to keep at 100%. This idea is grounded in evidence that closer relationships do involve **disproportionately more interaction and emotional energy** than weaker ties[\[14\]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-022-06066-1?error=cookies_not_supported&code=c48bb189-b1f1-43c4-8eab-bef7cb92c190#:~:text=,it%20is%20also%20costly%20in).

* **Need for Ongoing Maintenance:** A core principle is that **relationships are active processes** – they need regular **maintenance behaviors** (talking, spending time, sending support) to stay strong. If people stop investing time and energy, the relationship quality will **gradually decay rather than abruptly end**[\[15\]](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1090513810000966#:~:text=Finally%2C%20we%20focus%20on%20the,Thus%2C%20we%20took%20emotional%20closeness)[\[16\]](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1090513810000966#:~:text=particular%20level%20of%20emotional%20intensity,Specifically%2C%20we%20tested%20two%20hypotheses). Empirical studies confirm that **“out of sight, out of mind” is a real phenomenon**: when contact frequency drops, **closeness and emotional intensity decline** over time[\[4\]](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1090513810000966#:~:text=maintenance%20and%20decay,between%20kin%20relations%20and%20friendships). Therefore, the concept of a **depleting meter that you have to “recharge” by interactions** is backed by science. The app should encourage consistent small interactions (a message, a call, a meetup) as “maintenance boosts” to keep the relationship’s meter from hitting low levels. It’s worth noting that **friendships are particularly maintenance-dependent** – one study concluded friends require active upkeep to remain at a given level of intimacy, more so than familial ties[\[17\]](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1090513810000966#:~:text=keep%20the%20relationship%20at%20a,at%20a%20particular%20level%20of).

* **Reciprocity and Balance:** Your system might consider whether the engagement is **one-sided or mutual**. Healthy relationships typically involve **reciprocal initiative** – both people reach out to each other, share, and respond. In terms of design, if the app only tracks the user’s actions (e.g. how often *you* contacted your friend), it might miss if the friend never responds or vice versa. While harder to quantify, incorporating a notion of **balance** is useful. Perhaps the user can log whether an interaction was initiated by them or by the other person. Since **balanced exchange strengthens relationships[\[7\]](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1523506/full#:~:text=and%20settings%20%282%29,being%2C%20social%20interactions%2C%20and%20cognitive)**, the meter could reflect that truly strong ties have engagement from both sides. (For example, if you are always the one making contact, the relationship might be more fragile than if both parties regularly initiate.) In research on friendship quality, mutual confiding and equal exchange are seen as signs of a robust friendship[\[7\]](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1523506/full#:~:text=and%20settings%20%282%29,being%2C%20social%20interactions%2C%20and%20cognitive).

* **Quality of Interaction (Positive vs. Negative):** Not all interactions are equal in value. **Positive interactions** (having fun together, providing emotional support, expressing appreciation) **strengthen the relationship**, whereas **negative interactions** (conflict, criticism, unreliability) can damage it. Close relationships generally have a mix of positive and negative aspects, and both affect the overall quality[\[5\]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7485933/#:~:text=The%20quality%20of%20close%20relationships,and%20improved)[\[6\]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7485933/#:~:text=%28Mustafa%2C%20Carson,Rovillard%20%26%20Kuiper%2C%202011). A simple meter might focus on quantity of contact, but it’s important to remember **quality matters** too. For instance, a conversation that involves active listening and care will boost the relationship more than a brief, superficial chat – and a harsh argument could set the relationship back despite recent contact. While quantifying quality is challenging, your system might allow the user to note the sentiment of an interaction (e.g. tag it as positive or negative). At minimum, be aware from theory that **frequent contact alone isn’t enough – it should be mostly positive** to truly keep a relationship strong[\[6\]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7485933/#:~:text=%28Mustafa%2C%20Carson,Rovillard%20%26%20Kuiper%2C%202011).

* **Cognitive Limits and Personal Differences:** Research in social science suggests humans have a **cognitive limit on how many relationships they can actively maintain** (popularly known as Dunbar’s number, roughly 150 relationships, with smaller circles for closer friendships)[\[18\]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-022-06066-1?error=cookies_not_supported&code=c48bb189-b1f1-43c4-8eab-bef7cb92c190#:~:text=whose%20inclusive%20sizes%20follow%20an,relationships1%E2%80%94progressively%20devoting%20less%20to%20those). The limiting factor is time and mental resources – we only have so much attention to distribute among our social ties[\[19\]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-022-06066-1?error=cookies_not_supported&code=c48bb189-b1f1-43c4-8eab-bef7cb92c190#:~:text=from%20the%20perspective%20of%20the,explain%20differences%20in%20network%20size11). This has two implications for your app: First, users may need to **prioritize which relationships to actively nurture** (the app could help highlight which ones are most important or most at risk). Second, the app could remind users that it’s normal to not maintain an unlimited number of friends at full strength. Individuals also differ in their social energy: an extrovert might happily sustain dozens of contacts, whereas someone else might feel overwhelmed beyond a few close friends. The system should be customizable to the user’s personal capacity. In essence, **taking into account human limits on relationships** will make the app more realistic and user-friendly.

In summary, building a “relationship meter” grounded in science means recognizing that **relationships thrive on regular, reciprocal, and positive interactions**. The key variables – closeness, time, frequency, support, trust – all feed into the health of a relationship. Some people need help remembering to tend to these variables (hence your app), and different relationships will have different “settings” for how much maintenance they require. By considering relationship type, closeness level, interaction frequency, reciprocity, and interaction quality, you can create a system that reflects the **real dynamics observed in empirical research**[\[15\]](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1090513810000966#:~:text=Finally%2C%20we%20focus%20on%20the,Thus%2C%20we%20took%20emotional%20closeness)[\[16\]](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1090513810000966#:~:text=particular%20level%20of%20emotional%20intensity,Specifically%2C%20we%20tested%20two%20hypotheses) and helps users keep their relationships strong.
